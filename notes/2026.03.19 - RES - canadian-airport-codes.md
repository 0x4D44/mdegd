## 1. WHY DO CANADIAN AIRPORT CODES START WITH "Y"?

### The Historical Explanation

In the 1930s, Canada's Department of Transport assigned **two-letter identifiers** to weather reporting stations across the country. These were used by the meteorological service and aviation authorities for radio communication and weather reporting.

When the **IATA (International Air Transport Association)** standardised on **three-letter codes** for airports worldwide in the 1940s, Canada needed to convert its existing two-letter station identifiers into three-letter codes. The solution was simple: **prepend a letter** to the existing two-letter code.

The letter **"Y"** was chosen to indicate **"Yes"** — meaning the station **had a weather reporting station** co-located with an airport. Stations that did *not* have an associated airport received other prefix letters (like "W" for weather-only stations, or "X" for stations without weather services). So:

- **Y** = Yes, this weather station is at an airport
- **W** = Weather station only (no airport)
- Other letters for other categories

This is why nearly every Canadian airport code begins with **Y**: the "Y" literally meant "yes, there's an airport here."

### The Two-Letter Origins

The original two-letter codes were **not always obviously derived** from city names. Some were based on:
- Radio call signs
- Telegraph station identifiers
- Weather station designations
- Sometimes seemingly arbitrary assignments

When a two-letter code already began with Y, prepending another Y produced a **double-Y** code. This is why several major airports have "YY" at the start (e.g., YYZ, YYC, YYJ, YYT).

### How the IATA System Works

- IATA assigns **three-letter location identifiers** to airports, cities, and other travel-relevant locations worldwide
- They are distinct from **ICAO codes** (four-letter codes used by air traffic control — Canadian ICAO codes start with "CY", e.g., CYYZ for Toronto)
- IATA codes are primarily used for airline ticketing, baggage handling, and passenger-facing operations
- ICAO codes are used for flight planning, air traffic control, and meteorology

## 3. RUSH AND "YYZ" — THE SONG

### Key Facts

- **Song title:** "YYZ"
- **Band:** Rush (Canadian prog-rock trio from Toronto)
- **Album:** *Moving Pictures* (Rush's 8th studio album)
- **Released:** February 12, 1981
- **Record label:** Mercury Records / Anthem Records
- **Producer:** Terry Brown
- **Duration:** 4:24
- **Type:** **Instrumental** — no vocals
- **Songwriters:** Music by **Geddy Lee** (bass/keyboards) and **Alex Lifeson** (guitar); lyrics by **Neil Peart** (drums) — though as an instrumental, the "lyrics" credit refers to the conceptual/structural composition

### The Morse Code Connection

The song's iconic opening rhythm spells out **Y-Y-Z in Morse code**:

- **Y** = **-.--** (dash dot dash dash)
- **Y** = **-.--** (dash dot dash dash)
- **Z** = **--..** (dash dash dot dot)

The rhythm is played on Neil Peart's crotales (small tuned cymbals) and the guitar, creating the distinctive opening pattern. This Morse code sequence represents the **identifier signal** that would be transmitted by the YYZ VOR (VHF Omnidirectional Range) navigation beacon at Toronto Pearson airport.

Rush members heard this Morse code pattern constantly when flying home to Toronto — it's the signal that told pilots they were approaching Pearson. The song captures the **feeling of coming home**.

### Grammy Connection

"YYZ" was nominated for the **Grammy Award for Best Rock Instrumental Performance** at the **24th Grammy Awards (1982)**. It did not win — the award went to The Police for "Behind My Camel."

Rush never won a Grammy despite multiple nominations throughout their career.

### Neil Peart (1952-2020)

- Born **September 12, 1952**, in **Hamilton, Ontario**
- Died **January 7, 2020**, in **Santa Monica, California** (brain cancer — glioblastoma)
- Joined Rush in **1974**, replacing original drummer John Rutsey
- Widely regarded as one of the greatest rock drummers in history
- Also Rush's primary lyricist
- Inducted into the **Rock and Roll Hall of Fame** (2013, with Rush)
- **Officer of the Order of Canada**
- Also an accomplished author and motorcycle travel writer
- The Morse code rhythm in YYZ is a showcase of his technical precision

## 5. THE TWO-LETTER ORIGIN CODES — DETAILED EXPLANATION

### How the System Worked

1. **1930s:** Canada's Department of Transport (and the Meteorological Service) assigned **two-letter identifiers** to weather reporting stations
2. **1940s-1950s:** IATA standardised **three-letter codes** globally
3. **Conversion rule:** Canadian two-letter codes were expanded to three letters by adding a prefix:
   - **Y** = station co-located with an airport ("Yes, airport")
   - **W** = weather station only (no airport)
   - **U** = upper-air observation station (in some schemes)
   - Other letters for other station types

### Why Some Codes Have Double-Y

If the original two-letter station identifier **already started with Y**, then prepending Y created a double-Y:
- Station code **YZ** (Toronto) → Y + YZ → **YYZ**
- Station code **YC** (Calgary) → Y + YC → **YYC**
- Station code **YJ** (Victoria) → Y + YJ → **YYJ**
- Station code **YT** (St. John's) → Y + YT → **YYT**

### Derivation Certainty Levels

**High confidence** (the two-letter code clearly relates to the city):
- **OW** → OttaWa → **YOW**
- **WG** → WinniPeG → **YWG**
- **QB** → QueBec → **YQB**
- **FC** → Fredericton → **YFC**
- **SJ** → Saint John → **YSJ**
- **FB** → Frobisher Bay → **YFB**
- **VR** → VancouveR → **YVR**

**Medium confidence** (plausible but not definitively documented):
- **EG** → EdmontonG (or Edmonton Ground) → **YEG**
- **HZ** → HalifaX (Z substituting for X) → **YHZ**
- **UL** → possibly from the Dorval/Montreal station identifier → **YUL** (the "UL" origin is debated — some suggest it relates to the original radio identifier for the station near "ville-de-Dorval" area, but this is not confirmed with certainty)

**Low confidence / arbitrary** (no clear derivation from city name):
- **XE** → Saskatoon → **YXE** (no obvious connection)
- **XU** → London, Ontario → **YXU** (no obvious connection)
- **XY** → Whitehorse → **YXY** (no obvious connection)
- **YZ** → Toronto → **YYZ** (the YZ likely comes from the telegraph station call sign, not the city name)
- **ZF** → Yellowknife → **YZF** (no obvious connection)

## 6. HOW CANADIAN CODES COMPARE TO OTHER COUNTRIES

### United States
- US airport codes have **no standard prefix letter**
- Many major US airports inherited their codes from old weather station identifiers too, but without a standard prefix system
- Some US codes are obviously derived from city names: **LAX** (Los Angeles), **SFO** (San Francisco), **JFK** (New York — named for Kennedy), **ORD** (Chicago O'Hare — from old name "Orchard Field")
- Some are puzzling: **MCO** (Orlando — from "McCoy Air Force Base")
- US ICAO codes use the prefix **K** (e.g., KLAX, KJFK)

### United Kingdom
- UK airport codes have **no standard prefix**
- They are typically derived from airport or city names: **LHR** (London Heathrow), **LGW** (London Gatwick), **MAN** (Manchester), **EDI** (Edinburgh), **GLA** (Glasgow)
- UK ICAO codes use **EG** prefix (e.g., EGLL for Heathrow)

### Australia
- Australian codes have **no standard prefix**
- Usually derived from city names: **SYD** (Sydney), **MEL** (Melbourne), **BNE** (Brisbane), **PER** (Perth), **ADL** (Adelaide)
- Australian ICAO codes use **Y** prefix (e.g., YSSY for Sydney) — so confusingly, Australia's ICAO codes start with Y, while Canada's IATA codes start with Y!

### Other Notable Country Patterns
- **Russia:** ICAO codes start with **U** (e.g., UUEE for Moscow Sheremetyevo)
- **France:** ICAO codes start with **LF** (e.g., LFPG for Paris CDG)
- **Germany:** ICAO codes start with **ED** (e.g., EDDF for Frankfurt)

## 7. OBSCURE FACTS

### Canada's Airport Count
- Canada has approximately **1,425 airports** (as of recent data)
- This makes Canada **5th globally** for number of airports
- Behind: USA (~15,873), Brazil (~4,919), Australia (~2,180), Mexico (~1,485)
- The 26 major airports handle most of the traffic; hundreds of regional/remote airstrips serve northern communities

### Most Northerly Airport in Canada (and the World)
- **Alert Airport (YLT)** — latitude **82.52°N** on Ellesmere Island, Nunavut
- This is the **most northerly airport in the world**
- Alert itself is the **most northerly permanently inhabited place on Earth** (population: ~62 military/research personnel)
- Only about 817 km (508 miles) from the North Pole
- Established in **1950**; military airbase operated by DND / Canadian Armed Forces
- Gravel runway, 5,500 feet long
- ICAO code: CYLT

### Other Extreme Northern Airports
- **Eureka Airport (YEU)** — 79.99°N, Nunavut (weather station)
- **Grise Fiord Airport (YGZ)** — 76.43°N, Nunavut (most northerly Inuit community)

### Highest Altitude Airport in Canada
- **Banff Airport (YBA)** — 4,583 feet elevation, Alberta (in the Rocky Mountains)
- Compare to Denver International (5,431 feet) or La Paz, Bolivia (13,325 feet) — Canada's airports are not especially high

### Airport Codes That Have Changed (or Preserve Old Names)
- **YFB (Iqaluit)** — the city was called **Frobisher Bay** until **1987**, when it was renamed to its Inuktitut name Iqaluit ("place of fish"). The airport code YFB **preserves the old name** — FB = Frobisher Bay. Named for **Sir Martin Frobisher** (c. 1535-1594), English navigator who explored the Canadian Arctic in the 1570s looking for the Northwest Passage.
- **YUL (Montreal)** — the airport was called **Dorval International Airport** until **2004**, when it was renamed to **Montreal-Pierre Elliott Trudeau International Airport** after Canada's 15th Prime Minister (served 1968-1979 and 1980-1984, died 2000). The code YUL was not changed.
- **YYZ (Toronto)** — officially **Lester B. Pearson International Airport**, named after Canada's **14th Prime Minister** (served 1963-1968) who won the **Nobel Peace Prize in 1957** for helping resolve the Suez Crisis through UN peacekeeping. Previously known as **Malton Airport** (opened 1938). Sometimes still informally called "Malton" by older residents.
- **YXY (Whitehorse)** — renamed to **Erik Nielsen Whitehorse International Airport**, honouring **Erik Nielsen** (1924-2008), who was MP for Yukon and **Deputy Prime Minister** under Brian Mulroney. He was also the brother of actor **Leslie Nielsen** ("Surely you can't be serious...").
- **YXE (Saskatoon)** — named **Saskatoon John G. Diefenbaker International Airport** after **John Diefenbaker** (1895-1979), Canada's **13th Prime Minister** (1957-1963), who represented Prince Albert, Saskatchewan. Buried at the Diefenbaker Canada Centre in Saskatoon.

### Gander Airport (YQX) — Historic Significance
- **Gander International Airport** in Newfoundland was once one of the **most important airports in the world**
- In the early days of transatlantic aviation (1930s-1960s), aircraft couldn't fly non-stop across the Atlantic
- Gander served as a crucial **refuelling stop** for flights between North America and Europe
- During **9/11 (September 11, 2001)**, when US airspace was closed, **38 planes carrying ~6,700 passengers** were diverted to Gander — the town's population was only ~10,000. This story became the Broadway musical **"Come From Away"** (2017)

### Billy Bishop Toronto City Airport (YTZ)
- Located on Toronto Islands, right downtown
- Named after **Billy Bishop** (1894-1956), a WWI flying ace — the top Canadian air ace with 72 aerial victories
- One of the few city-centre airports in North America
